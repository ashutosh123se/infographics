01 · DEFINITION

What Is Ransomware?

Ransomware is a type of malicious software that blocks access to a victim's data or entire system — usually by encrypting files — and demands a ransom payment, typically in cryptocurrency, in exchange for the decryption key. It is a form of digital extortion: the attacker doesn't need to steal your data to profit, only to take it hostage.

Modern ransomware often adds a second lever called double extortion — attackers exfiltrate sensitive data before encrypting it, then threaten to leak it publicly if the ransom isn't paid, even if backups let the victim recover without paying.

WHO IT TARGETS

Hospitals, schools, city governments, manufacturers, and individual users alike — any environment where downtime is costly enough that paying feels like the "cheaper" option.

03 · HOW IT WORKS

The Ransomware Attack Lifecycle

Ransomware doesn't strike in one step — it moves through a predictable chain of stages. Understanding the chain is what makes defense possible: breaking it at any single link stops the attack.

1

Delivery & Initial Access

The attacker gets a foothold — most often through a phishing email with a malicious attachment or link, a compromised remote-desktop (RDP) login, or an unpatched, internet-facing vulnerability.

Example: WannaCry's initial variant spread via a malicious email attachment; later variants exploited an unpatched Windows network vulnerability directly, with no email needed.
2

Execution & Exploitation

Once inside, the malware exploits a software or protocol vulnerability to run code and gain deeper system access, often escalating privileges to move beyond a single machine.

Example: WannaCry exploited "EternalBlue," a flaw in Windows' SMB file-sharing protocol that Microsoft had already patched two months earlier — but many systems hadn't installed it.
3

Lateral Movement & Propagation

The malware spreads across the network — self-propagating "worm" ransomware can jump machine to machine with no user interaction at all, while other strains rely on stolen credentials.

Example: WannaCry behaved as a self-replicating worm, scanning for other vulnerable machines on the same network and internet-wide, infecting over 200,000 systems in roughly 24 hours.
4

Encryption (The Payload)

The ransomware encrypts files across local drives and connected network shares using strong cryptography, often also deleting backup shadow copies so recovery isn't trivial.

Example: WannaCry's payload — nicknamed "Wana Decrypt0r 2.0" — locked victims' files and changed extensions, rendering documents, images, and databases unusable.
5

Extortion & Ransom Demand

A ransom note appears with payment instructions and a countdown timer, threatening permanent data loss (or, increasingly, public leaks) if payment isn't made in time.

Example: WannaCry displayed a red ransom screen demanding $300–$600 in Bitcoin, with a countdown clock threatening to double the price, then destroy the decryption key.

May 7–12, 2021

If WannaCry showed ransomware could spread like a worm, Colonial Pipeline showed how a single weak login can cripple critical infrastructure. The DarkSide ransomware group didn't need a flashy exploit — just one unused VPN account with no multi-factor authentication.

MAY 6
Attackers gain access using a compromised password for an old VPN account that lacked multi-factor authentication — the password had leaked in an unrelated, prior data breach.
MAY 6–7

06 · OTHER NOTABLE RANSOMWARE ATTACKS

It Keeps Happening

WannaCry and Colonial Pipeline aren't isolated stories — they're part of a pattern that continues today, across every industry.

MAY 2021 · REvil
JBS Foods

The world's largest meat producer had U.S. and Australian plants shut down. JBS paid an $11 million ransom — down from REvil's initial $22.5 million demand.

FEB 2024 · ALPHV/BlackCat
Change Healthcare

Attackers used one Citrix remote-access account with no MFA to reach a company processing a third of all U.S. patient records. UnitedHealth paid $22 million; data on roughly 100 million people was exposed.

JULY 2021 · REvil
Kaseya Supply-Chain Attack

Attackers compromised Kaseya's IT-management software and pushed ransomware to as many as 1,500 downstream businesses through a single trusted software update.

07 · PREVENTION METHODS

Breaking the Chain

Every stage of the lifecycle above is a place to stop the attack. These are the controls that matter most.

✓ Patch promptly

Apply security updates as soon as they're released — WannaCry exploited a vulnerability Microsoft had already fixed two months earlier.

✓ Back up the 3-2-1 way

Keep 3 copies of critical data, on 2 different media, with 1 copy offline or immutable — so encrypted files can be restored without paying.

✓ Train people to spot phishing

Most ransomware still starts with a malicious email attachment or link — regular, realistic training reduces click-through rates.

✓ Segment the network

Limit how far malware can spread laterally by isolating critical systems and restricting unnecessary file-sharing protocols like SMB.

✓ Use endpoint detection & response

Modern EDR/antivirus tools can catch and quarantine ransomware behavior before mass encryption occurs.

✓ Enforce MFA & least privilege

Multi-factor authentication and limited admin rights make it much harder for stolen credentials to turn into a full breach.

✓ Have an incident response plan

Know in advance who to call, how to isolate infected machines, and how to restore from backup — tested before, not during, an incident.

✓ Don't assume paying works

Paying the ransom doesn't guarantee file recovery and funds further attacks — report incidents to authorities like CISA or local law enforcement instead.
